Mumbai —
the great housing paradox

This blog is my analysis of housing, particularly slum organisation and management in Mumbai. My insights are drawn from state and local policies stretching from last century until 2021. I used excerpts from Suketu Mehta’s Maximum City to weave real life stories and perceptions through a macrostructure of policies and governance decisions.

scroll

Mumbai introduced

Mumbai (renamed from Bombay in 1996) is one of the most significant cities of India and the Global South. Today, the financial, commercial, and entertainment capital of the country, it was originally a collection of seven fishing islands. After being reclaimed into a solid land from the sea only in 1845 by the British, Bombay witnessed an immense industrial growth till the late 20th century, attracting great influx of migrants around the nation.

The second most populous city in India, it is amongst the densest megacities on the planet. Over the last century, with its exploding population, the city, despite its geographical constraints have sprawls outwards and today covers the vast Mumbai Metropolitan Region (MMR) of 4,250 km².

With its linear growth along the coast in the beginning, the city can be divided into the main Greater Mumbai Island City and its suburbs on the north-east that gradually mushroomed in the last decades. The island city though covering 10% of MMR, houses a fourth of its population.

Apart from the issues of a typical megacity, Mumbai faces a peculiar problem of housing. It is not just the problem of space but a complex nexus of political, policy and legislative decisions that has produced this mammoth challenge of housing disparity in this city.

The housing situation of Mumbai is appalling at best. Presently, a third of its households live in slums. The island city is even worse — 80% of its houses are supplied informally.

Mumbai contains Dharavi, Asia’s largest slum, among its 2,400 other clusters. But these slums, due to their immense density, cover just over 8.5% of the island city’s area. Also, 55% of families live in a single room.

Due to a range of factors, the cost of land here is skyrocketing, making formal housing absolutely unaffordable for most citizens. An estimated three-quarters of all urban space is owned by only 6% of households, and just 91 people control the majority of all vacant land in Mumbai.

Meanwhile, over 250,000 houses — mainly built for higher and middle-income groups — lie unsold or vacant. Many are simply locked up to evade Mumbai’s unreasonable housing laws. These staggering contradictions are a result of policy decisions and legislations: some obsolete yet existing, many evolving in the emerging atmosphere of neoliberalism.

Mumbai’s social and physical fabric today is a result of complex layers of macro and micro policies, legislations and politics that have continued to weave and entangle for over seven decades since India’s independence in 1947.

1

Rent Control’s Unintended Consequences

1947 · 1999 · 2019

The Bombay Rents, Hotel and Lodging House Rates Control Act, 1947 was one of the most controversial pieces of legislation to affect the city’s built form. Passed as a reform to protect tenants from inflation and evictions after WWII, it froze rents in the island city for the next fifty years — only slightly amended in 1999.

Over time, the real value of that rent tends toward zero. Tenants never move out. Landlords — who still hold the right to sell entire buildings under rent control — seldom do, for an obvious lack of demand. The older and more decrepit a building is, the less likely it is to be sold, rebuilt or maintained. A significant portion of the housing stock sits unmaintained and underused on valuable land, with virtually no addition to rental housing — especially for low-income groups — for decades (S Patel, 2005).

Rent Act has become a sensitive, untouchable issue, backed by a large tenant lobby — a vote bank that has blocked appropriate reform.

Cotton Mills and the Lands Ceiled

1976 · 1980s · 1991 · 2001 · 2007 · 2008

1.33Island-city FSI limit — among the world’s lowest
1.24 m²Open space per person (Mumbai)
9 m²WHO standard for open space

The once thriving cotton textile industry declined in the 1980s, leaving about 2.5 km² of under- and unused land right in the heart of the city. But the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act — ULCRA, 1976 and the Floor Space Index (FSI) restrictions under the city’s Development Control Regulations made redevelopment of these parcels impossible, causing an artificial scarcity of land.

ULCRA limited private land ownership to just 2,000 m². Mill owners could not sell without surrendering a large portion of land for public uses — and instead of bringing land to market, the Act locked it into legal disputes over acquisition and compensation.

Only in 1991, after a revision of the DCRs, was redevelopment permitted — a third of each parcel reserved for open space, a third for social housing, and a third for private development. But mill owners protested for more land for luxury apartments and commercial use. The judgment favoured the mill workers, yet ultimately reduced the possibility of new affordable housing still further.

ULCRA was repealed in 2007, and FSI/TDR incentives arrived in 2008 — by then, the mill lands had already become luxury real estate, not housing for the city.

3

Say Yes to Sprawls — Metropolitan Regional Plans

1970–1991 · 1996–2011 · 2016–2036

As a response to growing air pollution, land-use polarization and congestion, the MMRDA formulated its first Regional Plan in 1970, proposing well-intentioned strategies that eventually turned counterproductive. Instead of redeveloping the island’s underused parcels — such as the mills’ land — the plan froze FSI to 1.33 and pushed the city outwards to decentralize growth.

New counter-magnets like Navi Mumbai and the Bandra–Kurla complex attracted middle-income housing and slum redevelopment projects. But the draconian land-use policy, coupled with ULCRA 1976 and the Rent Control Act 1947, made new investment almost impossible on the island — artificially escalating land values and gentrifying the middle class and the poor outward or into slums.

After 1991, FSI restrictions were eased through Transferable Development Rights (TDR) — but never as a deliberate spatial policy; it was offered to only a few individual plots in exchange for financial resources from the developer (A Bertaud, 2011). Five decades of regressive land-use policy created the conditions of a zero-sum game for the consumption of floor space — and it continues to do so.

Slums — Bulldozed but not Built Back… Then Solved, Conditions Applied

1894 · 1956 · 1991 · 2015

Phase A

Bulldozed — negation, tolerance, acceptance (1956–1985)

Prior to liberalization, the Bombay Municipal Corporation identified itself as a controller, provider and facilitator. It treated slums through “improvement schemes” — new public housing on public lands, and demolition of slum areas without alternative accommodation for those evicted. Policy moved through phases of negation (1950s–60s), tolerance (1970s) and acceptance (1980s) (Indorewala, 2018).

Armed with the Land Acquisition Act of 1894 (amended 1964), the BMC carried out comprehensive renewal under the Slum Clearance and Improvement Act, 1956 and various Slum Improvement Programmes (SIP 1970, Vacant Lands Act 1975, LISPG 1985) — evicting dwellers on short or no notice, without compensation, “resettling” them far from their workplaces. In the last century, BMC demolished more houses than it built.

India’s Planning Commission declared in 1983: “there is overwhelming evidence to show that efforts to produce affordable housing for the poor by government bodies have failed.”

“The squad is supposed to give a seven-day legal notice asking for documentation that the structure is legal… But ‘the staff is under great fear.’ And there is the money: ‘if the notice is issued, the entire file will be sold for a lakh or two to the party concerned.’”

Phase B

Solved — conditions applied (1991–2015)

With neoliberal structural adjustment from the 1980s onward, the state shifted from provider to facilitator for private actors. The Slum Redevelopment Scheme 1991, Slum Rehabilitation Authority 1995, State Housing Policy 2007, Rajiv Awas Yojana 2009 and Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana 2015 all adopted an “enabling markets” strategy — cross-subsidy development on slum land and subsidized home loans. They remain unsuccessful in improving affordability or easing sky-high island land values.

Housing came to be reconceived as an economic good, not a public good. The dream of Mumbai as a “world class” city pathologized and criminalized its slums. Between 2004–05 alone, the Government bulldozed over 100,000 homes across the island in the name of public purpose and encroachment in the No Development Zone (Indorewala, 2018).

5

Shiv Sena — An Alternate Governance

1966 · 1985–90 · 1995–99 · 2014–19

Mumbai’s housing situation is also peculiarly shaped by its regional party Shiv Sena — a right-wing Hindu nationalist party established in 1966 and periodically in power. It has advocated the Right to the City — but only for its vote bank, the Marathi manus. The party runs a robust grassroots network of volunteers and local service delivery penetrating every end of the city, especially its slums.

It functions as an alternative form of local governance — ensuring housing formally when in power, and informally anyway. Slum dwellers, mostly Hindu and Marathi, largely rely on Shiv Sena and its decentralized shakhas for basic services and alternate administration (Saunders, 2012). While its assistance alleviates their struggle to a degree, it fails the larger picture of citizens demanding formal, proper housing from the actual concerned authority.

Conclusion — the whole chronology lit

Every event · every layer · 1930–2020

A city’s physical form is not merely a product of its municipal land-use plan and regional strategies, but a very intricate synthesis of decisions made by a range of actors over space and time.

On plotting the chronology of Mumbai’s political economy — its legislation, policies and politics — one can really comprehend what the ripple effect of these decisions and events could be. In Mumbai, the housing crisis continues to worsen: older decisions, though made with good intentions, ended up causing immense damage — and are still not acknowledged and revised — while newer forces emerge, further adding to the complexities.
